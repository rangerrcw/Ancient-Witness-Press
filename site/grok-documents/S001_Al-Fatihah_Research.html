S001 — AL-FĀTIḤAH (THE OPENING)

Canonical position: Surah 1 of 114

Verses in standard Kufan count: 7 (basmala counted as v.1)

Traditional chronology: Meccan — commonly listed among the earliest; exact serial number in revelation lists varies (often ~5th). Certainty: traditional, not internally dated by the surah.

Checkpoint: Complete short-surah packet (CUM-00 system test)

Evidence date: 10 September 2026

1. Surah Overview

Al-Fatihah is a short liturgical unit of praise and petition. It names God, ascribes praise and mercy and eschatological authority to God, then switches from third-person description to second-person address: exclusive worship and help-seeking, then a request for guidance on a path defined by favor and by two rejected alternatives (those subjected to anger; those who go astray).

The surah does not narrate an event, name Muhammad, name Jesus, name earlier books, or issue a legal ruling. Later Islamic practice treats it as the required opening of ritual prayer. That liturgical use is Level 3–4 (early/classical tradition), not a Level 1 statement inside these seven verses.

2. Structural Analysis

Two-movement structure is the most economical reading of the Arabic:

A. vv.1–4 — Identification and praise of God (basmala + hamd + mercy + eschatological sovereignty).

B. vv.5–7 — Address to God: exclusive worship and aid; petition for guidance; triple path-classification (favored / angered-upon / astray).

v.5 is the hinge: iyyaka (“You [alone]”) fronted twice. Classical tafsir often splits the surah as “half for God, half for the servant.” That split is a later pedagogical frame (Level 4) that maps reasonably onto the grammar but is not stated by the text.

Verse-count dispute: most printed muṣḥafs following the Kufan tradition count the basmala as 1:1 and close at seven verses. Some other counting traditions treat the basmala as a header and split v.7, still totaling seven. This is a recitation/codex convention issue, not a different wording of the prayer body. Classification: OPEN on which count is “original”; DIRECT that the wording block is stable.

3. Verse-by-Verse Investigation

1:1 bismi llāhi l-raḥmāni l-raḥīm

“In the name of Allah, the Raḥmān, the Raḥīm.” Formula of invocation. Allah is the proper name used throughout the Quran for God. Al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm share root r-ḥ-m (mercy; also raḥim, womb). Traditional distinction: Raḥmān as expansive/universal mercy, Raḥīm as enduring or particular mercy. That distinction is classical exegesis (Level 4), widely repeated, not parsed by 1:1 itself. DIRECT: God is named and two mercy epithets are applied.

1:2 al-ḥamdu li-llāhi rabbi l-ʿālamīn

“The praise is for Allah, rabb of the ʿālamīn.” Al-ḥamd is praise/commendation with the definite article (the praise, not merely a praise). Rabb covers lord, owner, fosterer, master — not only “deity.” ʿĀlamīn is plural of ʿālam (world/realm/class of beings). English “worlds / universe / all beings” all sit inside the semantic range. DIRECT: praise is ascribed to Allah as rabb of the ʿālamīn.

1:3 al-raḥmāni l-raḥīm

Repeats the two mercy names without Allah. Reinforces mercy after lordship. DIRECT repetition. SYNTHESIS: mercy is not a minor attribute in this opening unit.

1:4 māliki / maliki yawmi l-dīn

Two canonical readings of the first word exist: mālik (owner/possessor) and malik (king). Both are among the accepted qirāʾāt. Yawm al-dīn: dīn here is widely read as requital/judgment rather than “religion” in the modern institutional sense, though dīn’s Quranic range includes religion, debt, custom, and requital. Immediate phrase is eschatological. DIRECT: God is mālik or malik of the Day of dīn. OPEN: whether the authorial/performative intention was one reading only.

1:5 iyyāka naʿbudu wa-iyyāka nastaʿīn

Object fronted twice with iyyāka (“You”). Naʿbudu from ʿ-b-d (serve/worship/enslave oneself to). Nastaʿīn from ʿ-w-n (seek help). First-person plural. The grammar strongly marks exclusivity of the object; English “alone” is an interpretive tightening that most translators add because of the fronting. DIRECT: the speakers worship and seek help from the addressee. STRONG PATTERN (grammar): exclusivity of that object. The speakers are not identified by name.

1:6 ihdinā l-ṣirāṭa l-mustaqīm

“Guide us (to/on) the straight path.” Ihdinā is a request for hidāyah (guidance). Ṣirāṭ is a path/road (loanword likely from Latin via Greek/Aramaic strata of late antique Near East — linguistic history, not a Level 1 theological claim). Mustaqīm: straight, upright, correct. DIRECT: the speakers ask God to guide them on a straight path.

1:7 ṣirāṭa lladhīna anʿamta ʿalayhim ghayri l-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim wa-lā l-ḍāllīn

The path is defined by three groups: (1) those You have favored; (2) not those angered-upon; (3) nor those who go astray. Anʿamta is second-person: God is the subject of favor. Al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim is a passive participle plus preposition: “those upon whom anger has fallen.” The text does not name the agent of anger in this clause, though the surah’s addressee is God. Al-ḍāllīn: those who stray/err. DIRECT: three-way classification of paths/people. The Quran in this surah does not name Jews, Christians, or any ethnicity. Identification of group (2) with Jews and group (3) with Christians is a widespread traditional tafsir claim (Level 3–4) and is audited as QCA-0002.

6. Theological Findings

God is one addressee, merciful, praiseworthy, lord of the worlds, and eschatological judge/owner. Worship and help-seeking are directed to that addressee. Human need for guidance is assumed. Moral/religious space is divided into favored, angered-upon, and astray. Salvation language in the later systematic sense (atonement, justification) is not present. Intercession is not mentioned. Muhammad is not mentioned.

8. Biblical Cross-References

Genre-level resemblance, not quotation:

Psalmic praise of the LORD as king and refuge (e.g. Psalm 24; 86; 145). Petition “teach me thy way / lead me” (Psalm 25; 27). The Lord’s Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13 / Luke 11:2–4): address to God, name/hallowing, petition for divine action, moral path. These are structural and thematic analogies. S001 does not cite them. No shared wording that would demonstrate literary dependence has been established here.

9. Agreement / Difference Analysis

AGREEMENT (limited, thematic): a merciful, praiseworthy God; prayer for guidance; coming judgment. PARTIAL AGREEMENT with biblical prayer: communal petition to one God, without the Lord’s Prayer’s “Father” language and without S001’s three-group path formula. OMISSION: no Jesus, no Spirit, no covenant name YHWH, no Israel, no church. DIRECT CONTRADICTION: none declared from S001 alone. See QB-0001.

10. Historical Context

If the traditional Meccan early dating is accepted, the unit is a short monotheistic prayer in a polytheistic cultic environment. The text itself does not date itself. Pre-Islamic Arabic already used Allah for a high god; the Quranic move is to attach exclusive worship and eschatological ownership to that name. Certainty on extra-textual setting: traditional + historically plausible; not proven by S001 wording.

21. Executive Surah Summary

Al-Fatihah, on its own wording, is a monotheistic praise-petition: Allah is lord of the worlds, merciful, and sovereign of the Day of requital; the speakers worship and seek help from that God alone and ask to be guided on the path of those God has favored, not the path of the angered-upon or the astray. It does not name the Prophet, Jesus, earlier scripture, or any people. Later tradition supplies liturgical function and a common identification of the last two groups; those supplies are recorded, not merged into Level 1.
