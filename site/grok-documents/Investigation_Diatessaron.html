LIVING SNAPSHOT — THE DIATESSARON

Pair with: Investigation_Tawrat_Injil_7_157.docx

IDs: QDISC-0374–0378; QB-0216–0219

Date: 12 September 2026

1. What it is

Greek title: dia tessarōn — through the four. Syriac name in the churches that used it: ewangeliyon da-mḥallṭē, the Gospel of the Mixed, set against ewangeliyon da-mparršē, the Gospel of the Separated (the four read apart).

Compiler: Tatian, a second-century Assyrian/Syrian Christian, pupil of Justin Martyr, author of an Oration to the Greeks. Date: mid-to-late second century. Language of first composition is disputed (Syriac or Greek). What is not disputed: the work weaves Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John into one running life of Jesus.

It is not a fifth source. Studies of the Arabic harmony and of Codex Fuldensis show Tatian used the great majority of the four (on one count, on the order of three-quarters of their combined verses). Markan special material is present. Johannine prologue is present — later Syriac writers note that it began ‘In the beginning was the Word.’

2. Why it matters for the Injil file

The Quranic loanword injīl almost certainly entered Arabic from Christian Aramaic/Syriac ewangeliyon. In the Syriac churches of the late second through fifth centuries, the book one heard in church as ‘the Gospel’ was often this mixed singular, not four separate lections. Aphrahat cites gospel material in mixed form. Ephrem wrote a commentary on the Mixed Gospel (Syriac original recovered in Chester Beatty Syriac 709; also Armenian). The Teaching of Addai treats a Diatessaron-type gospel as the Edessene church’s book. Theodoret of Cyrrhus (5th c.) says he pulled more than two hundred copies from churches in his diocese and replaced them with the four separated gospels.

So: a Christian in the Syriac orbit could honestly say ‘the Gospel’ in the singular and mean one bound narrative. That is the closest historical analogue to the Quran’s singular Injīl as a book-in-hand.

3. What it does not do

It does not become a book given to Jesus. Tatian compiled it a century and more after Jesus. It is still a life of Jesus written about him, not a kitāb sent down to him to recite.

It does not omit the passion and resurrection to match 4:157. The Dura Europos fragment (before 256 CE), widely taken as Diatessaronic, is a crucifixion-and-burial weave from the four. Ephrem comments the passion. The Arabic Diatessaron (11th c. translation from Syriac, already assimilated toward the Peshitta) still runs arrest, trial, crucifixion, burial, empty tomb. A singular Gospel in Syriac is not a non-crucifixion Gospel.

It does not contain a named finding of Muhammad. It is the four, reordered. 7:157’s written-finding problem is not solved by mixing the same four into one volume.

It was not the only gospel book in Arabia. By the seventh century the Peshitta four-gospel set was the standard Syriac Bible. Theodoret’s confiscations show the Mixed form was already being retired in the fifth century in some sees. What a given Najrānī or Ḥīran Christian held in 610 is not recoverable as ‘always the Diatessaron.’
